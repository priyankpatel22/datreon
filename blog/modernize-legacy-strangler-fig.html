Legacy Modernization

Strangler Fig, Not Big Bang: Modernizing Without Betting the Company

Datreon · June 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Every aging system reaches the point where someone says the words: "Let's just rebuild it." It's the most natural instinct in software — and one of the most dangerous. The full rewrite is where modernization budgets go to die. There's a safer way in, and it doesn't require betting the business on a single flip of a switch.

The legacy system is slow, fragile, and understood by fewer people every year. The pressure to replace it is real. So the plan becomes: build a shiny new version alongside the old one, and when it's finished, switch over. Clean, decisive, and — far too often — a disaster.

Why big-bang rewrites fail

A full rewrite fails for structural reasons, not for lack of talent:

  • You get no value until the very end. For months or years, you're spending heavily and shipping nothing. Momentum and patience run out before the finish line.
  • You're chasing a moving target. The old system doesn't freeze while you rebuild — it keeps changing to meet the business. Your new version is always one step behind the thing it's replacing.
  • The old system is the spec. Years of edge cases and quiet bug-fixes live only in that code. A rewrite rediscovers them the hard way — in production.
  • Cutover is all-or-nothing. One enormous switch, one enormous risk. If anything's wrong, there's no small rollback — only a crisis.

The strangler fig approach

The alternative borrows its name from a tree that grows around a host and gradually replaces it. Instead of rebuilding everything and switching once, you put a stable entry point in front of the old system and replace it one capability at a time — routing each migrated piece to new code while everything else keeps running untouched. The legacy system shrinks steadily until, one day, nothing is left running on it.

Each migrated capability ships and delivers value on its own. If a slice goes wrong, you roll back that one slice — not the whole program. And because the old and new run side by side, you can compare their outputs on real traffic before you trust the new path.

How it works in practice

  • Put a facade in front. Route all traffic through a single stable entry point (an API gateway, a routing layer). Users and integrations never see what's changing behind it.
  • Carve off one capability. Pick a well-bounded slice — ideally high-pain or high-change — and build just that in the new system.
  • Route it over, watch it, keep the fallback. Send that capability's traffic to the new code while the legacy version stays ready. Compare, monitor, and only then retire the old path.
  • Repeat until the host is empty. Each pass shrinks the legacy footprint until it holds nothing and can be switched off quietly — no dramatic cutover.

Big bang vs. strangler, side by side

Dimension Big-bang rewrite Strangler (incremental)
Time to first value Only when the whole thing ships With the first migrated capability
Risk profile All-or-nothing at the end Contained to one slice at a time
Rollback Enormous, if it's possible at all Per-slice and cheap
The old system Frozen in theory, still changing in reality Shrinks steadily until it's gone
Business disruption Concentrated at one high-stakes cutover Spread thin and largely invisible

When a rewrite is the right call

Incremental isn't dogma. Occasionally the old system is small enough, or so fundamentally mismatched to what you now need, that a clean rebuild is genuinely faster and safer. The test is honest scope: if you can rebuild and cut over in weeks, not quarters, a focused rewrite may win. The moment the timeline stretches into "a year of building in the dark," the strangler approach almost always beats it — because it turns one enormous bet into a series of small, reversible ones.

Modernization rarely fails because the new technology wasn't good enough. It fails because the plan asked an organization to hold its breath for too long. Replace the system the way it was actually built — one capability at a time — and you keep the lights on, keep delivering value, and keep the risk small the whole way through.
