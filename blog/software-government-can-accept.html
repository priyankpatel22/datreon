A Small Business's Guide to Delivering Software the Government Can Actually Accept

Datreon · October 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Plenty of small firms can write good code for a government program. Fewer know what it takes to get that code formally accepted — and the gap between the two is where timelines slip, invoices sit unpaid, and contracts don't get renewed.

A program office doesn't accept software because it works on your laptop in the demo. It accepts software because a specific, documented process says it's allowed to — and that process was almost never written by engineers. It was written by people worried about security, accessibility, continuity of operations, and what happens eighteen months from now when your team isn't the one maintaining it.

None of that is a mystery once you've been through it. It's also not something most small businesses learn until they hit it the hard way, mid-contract, with a delivery deadline already in motion.

Code is not the deliverable

The functional software is maybe a third of what "acceptance" actually checks. The rest is the evidence trail around it: proof that the required security controls are implemented, proof that it's usable by people with disabilities, proof that someone besides your team can operate it, and a written agreement — signed before you started — about what "done" even means.

Teams that treat the artifacts as an afterthought end up writing them under deadline pressure, badly, after the code is already finished. Teams that treat them as part of the build produce better software and clear acceptance faster, because the documentation forces you to be honest about gaps while there's still time to close them.

The acceptance criteria no one wrote down

The single most common failure mode isn't a missing control — it's a missing agreement. "Build us a dashboard that tracks X" is not acceptance criteria. A government customer, like any customer, will discover requirements during review that were never stated during scoping, and on a government contract that discovery can stall payment and extend the period of performance.

Write the acceptance test plan with the program office before you build, not after — specific scenarios, specific data, a specific person who signs off. It feels like overhead early. It's the thing that keeps "finished" from becoming a negotiation.

Security controls aren't optional homework

Most federal and many state systems require an Authority to Operate before anything touches real data in a real environment — a formal, documented risk decision made by an accountable official, backed by evidence that the required controls (access control, encryption, logging, incident response, and more, depending on the system's risk categorization) are actually implemented, not just described in a slide.

The System Security Plan is the document that carries that evidence. It's tedious to write and nearly impossible to retrofit convincingly after the fact. Build it alongside the system, control by control, and the ATO package becomes a formality instead of a scramble.

Accessibility is a requirement, not a nice-to-have

Section 508 compliance — keyboard navigation, screen-reader support, sufficient contrast, correctly labeled form fields — is a legal requirement for most federal systems, not a stretch goal reviewers will waive if the rest of the product is strong. It's also one of the few acceptance requirements you can test yourself, early, with free tools and a keyboard, instead of waiting for someone else to find the gaps.

Most 508 failures aren't exotic. They're a modal that traps keyboard focus, an icon-only button with no label, or color as the only signal for an error state. Catch those in a design review, not a government accessibility audit three weeks before go-live.

Documentation that outlives the contract

Government software is built to be operated past the life of your contract, often by people who weren't in the room when it was designed. A runbook that assumes institutional memory isn't really a runbook. Write down what a competent stranger would need: how to deploy it, how to tell if it's healthy, what to do when it isn't, and where the data actually lives.

Here's the shortlist worth treating as part of the build, not paperwork for the end:

Requirement What it actually means
System Security Plan Written, control-by-control proof of how security is implemented — not a claim that it is
Authority to Operate A formal risk decision by an accountable official before the system can go live with real data
Section 508 accessibility Keyboard and screen-reader usable, with a documented conformance report (VPAT)
Written acceptance test plan Specific pass/fail scenarios, agreed and signed before delivery — not discovered during review
Operations & runbook docs Enough detail that a team that didn't build the system can run and maintain it
Data handling rules Explicit, written answers on where data lives, who can access it, and how it's retired

Build acceptance in from day one

None of this is a reason for a small firm to avoid government work — it's a reason to plan for it correctly from the kickoff meeting, not the delivery deadline. Scope the controls, the accessibility review, and the acceptance test plan alongside the feature list, and "acceptance" stops being a gate at the end of the project and becomes a byproduct of how you built it.

The firms that deliver government software smoothly aren't the ones with the fastest engineers. They're the ones who stopped treating the paperwork as separate from the product.
