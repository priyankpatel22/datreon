What “AI Readiness” Really Means for a Federal Program Office

Datreon · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Every program office has heard the mandate by now: get AI ready. Almost none of them have been told what that actually means. It isn't a model, a pilot, or a vendor briefing — it's governance, oversight, and risk framing done before a single prompt touches real data.

"AI readiness" has become one of those phrases that sounds like a checkbox and behaves like a compliance obligation nobody wrote down. For a commercial team, getting it wrong means a disappointing pilot. For a federal program office, getting it wrong means a finding, a frozen rollout, or worse — a system that quietly makes decisions nobody can explain to an IG.

The good news: readiness isn't mysterious. It's five concrete questions a program office can answer honestly, long before anyone writes a line of model code.

Readiness starts with data governance, not data science

Most AI conversations start with the model. In government, that's backwards. The first question is never "which model" — it's "what are we allowed to do with this data, and can we prove it?" That means knowing where the data lives, what authorities govern it (Privacy Act, FISMA categorization, agency-specific handling rules), who's permitted to see it, and whether a vendor or a hosted model is even allowed to touch it under current authorities.

A surprising number of "AI projects" stall here — not because the use case was bad, but because nobody had mapped the data's governance boundary before the pilot started. Map it first. It's unglamorous and it's the actual foundation everything else sits on.

Authority to Operate doesn't pause for AI

An AI capability riding on a federal information system still needs to fit inside that system's security boundary — which means it still needs to go through ATO, or a documented path to one, before it touches production data. AI doesn't get a carve-out from FISMA, NIST 800-53 controls, or your agency's RMF process just because it's new and exciting. Treating it as exempt is how a promising pilot becomes a program office's next audit finding.

The practical move is to loop your ISSO and Authorizing Official in at the design stage, not the deployment stage. A capability scoped from day one to fit inside an existing boundary — or a clearly defined new one — moves faster than one that gets bolted on and then has to be retrofitted into compliance.

Not every use case carries the same risk — treat it that way

One of the fastest ways to stall a program is to frame every AI use case as equally risky. An internal tool that helps staff draft a first pass of a routine memo is not the same risk category as a system that influences a benefits determination or a safety-critical decision. Programs that conflate the two end up either over-governing a low-stakes tool into irrelevance, or under-governing a high-stakes one into a real problem.

Separate candidates by two honest questions: how much value does this create, and how much harm does it cause if it's wrong? Plot your actual candidates on that basis before committing resources to any of them.

Human oversight is a design decision, not a fallback

"A human reviews it" is not a plan unless someone has specified what the human is actually checking, how often, and what happens when they disagree with the system. Oversight that exists only on a slide doesn't hold up under an actual error. Build the review step into the workflow itself — who sees the output before it's acted on, what triggers escalation, and how disagreements get logged — before the capability goes live, not after the first incident.

Start with the boring, low-risk use case

The program offices that make real progress rarely start with the most ambitious use case on the list. They start with something unglamorous: drafting a first-pass summary of a long record for a human to edit, triaging routine correspondence, or searching internal documentation faster. Low-stakes, high-frequency, and reviewable — a place to build the governance muscle before anything touches a consequential decision.

Dimension Not ready Ready to start
Data governance Nobody can say what authority covers this data Handling rules and permissions are documented and known
ATO / compliance Treated as a separate track to deal with later ISSO and AO engaged at design time, boundary scoped
Risk framing Every use case treated as equally sensitive Candidates ranked by value and harm-if-wrong
Human oversight "A human reviews it" with no defined check Specific reviewer, review point, and escalation path
First use case The most ambitious, highest-stakes idea in the room Low-stakes, high-frequency, reviewable

None of this requires exotic technology or a new procurement vehicle to get started. It requires a program office willing to answer five honest questions before the first pilot kicks off — and a delivery partner who treats your ATO, your RMF, and your oversight obligations as part of the build, not paperwork to handle after the fact.
