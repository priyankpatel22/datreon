Before You Buy an AI Tool: A 5-Question Readiness Check

Datreon · October 7, 2026 · 6 min read

Every AI vendor demo looks good. That's not a signal — it's the one thing every demo is optimized for. Before a contract gets signed or an internal build gets greenlit, there are five questions that matter far more than anything in the pitch deck, and none of them are about the model.

A leadership team sits through a polished walkthrough, the tool answers a question correctly, and the room decides it's ready to buy. Six months later the project is stalled, the data team is fighting with the integration, and nobody can say whether the tool is actually helping. The tool wasn't the problem. The questions that should have been asked before the contract never got asked.

None of these questions require technical depth. They require someone willing to say "I don't know" out loud in a room full of people nodding along — and then go find out before money moves.

The checklist that replaces the sales pitch

Run every candidate tool or build through the same five gates, in the same order. If a vendor or an internal champion can't answer one cleanly, that's the finding — not a reason to assume it'll work out.

1. What decision does this actually change?

Every AI tool should be able to finish the sentence: "Right now, a person decides X by doing Y; this tool changes that to Z." If the honest answer is "it gives people more information to think about," that's not a decision changing — it's a dashboard with extra steps. Tools earn their budget by removing a specific, named bottleneck: a triage decision, a drafting task, a classification step. If nobody can name the decision, there's nothing yet to automate.

2. Is the data actually reachable?

A model is only as good as what it can see. "We have the data somewhere" is not the same as "the tool can query it, in the right format, with the right permissions, today." Find out where the relevant data lives, who can grant access to it, and what shape it's in before the pilot starts — not after the first disappointing result. Most AI projects that stall do so here, quietly, long before anyone blames the model.

3. How will you measure "right"?

If the plan for evaluating the tool is "we'll know it when we see it," there is no plan. A usable readiness check needs a concrete definition of correct: a held-out set of real examples with known answers, a tolerance for how often "close enough" counts, and a way to re-check accuracy after launch, not just once at demo time. Without a measurable bar, every result looks like success, because there's nothing it could fail against.

4. What happens when it's confidently wrong?

Every model is wrong sometimes, and the dangerous failures are the confident ones — the answer that's incorrect but doesn't look uncertain. Ask what the blast radius is when that happens: does a human review it before it reaches a customer or a decision? Is there a way to catch a pattern of errors before it compounds? A tool with no failure plan isn't more reliable than one that's honest about its limits — it's just more surprising.

5. Who owns it after launch?

The sign-off meeting is not the finish line. Models drift, data sources change shape, and usage patterns shift in ways nobody predicted at launch. If no specific person or team is accountable for watching accuracy, retraining or re-prompting, and deciding when to pull the plug, the tool will quietly degrade in the background until someone notices the hard way. "IT will handle it" is not an owner — a named person with the authority to act is.

The question that kills the most bad AI purchases isn't "can it do this?" It's "what happens the first time it's wrong, and who's watching for it?"

Turning the five questions into a decision

Score each candidate tool against the same table before any contract or build sprint. A vendor who gets defensive about any one row has told you something useful.

Question Good answer Red flag
1. Decision A named decision, with a current owner and process, that the tool replaces or speeds up "It gives better visibility" with no decision attached
2. Data Specific systems, confirmed access, known format, in hand before the pilot starts "We'll figure out the data during onboarding"
3. Metric A held-out test set and a numeric accuracy bar, checked before and after launch "We'll know it's working when people like it"
4. Failure A defined human-review step and a way to catch repeated errors No plan — or "the model is usually right"
5. Owner A named person accountable for accuracy and the call to shut it off "IT will keep an eye on it"

None of this is a reason to slow-walk every AI decision into committee. It's the opposite — a fast, five-question gate that lets a confident "yes" move immediately and catches the tools that were never going to work before they cost a budget cycle finding out. The vendors and internal champions with real answers will welcome the questions. The ones without them are telling you the readiness check already found its answer.
