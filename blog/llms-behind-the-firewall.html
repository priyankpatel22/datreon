LLMs Behind the Firewall: Integrating AI Without Leaking Your Data

Datreon · September 30, 2026 · 7 min read

The question isn't whether a language model can help with your data. It's where that data goes while it's helping. For teams handling contracts, health records, financial statements, or anything under an NDA, that question — not the model's accuracy — is what stalls the project.

It's a reasonable thing to get stuck on. "Just call the API" sounds simple until someone in security or legal asks the obvious follow-up: whose servers is our data touching, and what happens to it after the response comes back? Too many teams either skip the question and expose themselves, or let the question kill the project entirely. Neither is necessary. There are several legitimate ways to put an LLM to work on sensitive data, each with a real, well-understood tradeoff — not a leap of faith.

The question every security team asks first

Every LLM integration boils down to one design decision: does your data leave your boundary to reach the model, or does the model come to your data? Everything else — cost, latency, capability, operational burden — follows from that choice. It's worth answering deliberately, in writing, before any code gets written, rather than discovering the answer during a security review three weeks before launch.

Three ways to run an LLM, and where your data goes

In practice, teams choose from three deployment shapes. They aren't mutually exclusive — a mature program often uses more than one — but each has a distinct trust boundary.

Hosted API with a data processing agreement. You call a commercial model provider's API. Your data crosses your boundary to reach it, but a signed agreement contractually commits the provider to specific handling: no training on your inputs, defined retention windows, encryption in transit and at rest, and audit rights. This is the fastest path to a working system, and for most business data it's entirely defensible — provided the agreement actually says what you need it to say.

Private or VPC-hosted deployment. The same class of model, but run inside a cloud tenancy you control — a virtual private cloud, a dedicated instance, or a provider's "enterprise" offering that never routes through shared infrastructure. Your data still touches a vendor's model weights, but it never leaves a network boundary you administer. This suits regulated data that can live in your cloud environment but can't be handed to a third party outright.

Self-hosted open-weight models. You run the model yourself, on your own infrastructure, with no outbound call at all. Nothing leaves. This is the right answer when the data legally cannot leave your environment — certain government, defense, or health workloads — or when a provider's terms simply won't satisfy your obligations. It's also the most expensive option to run well: you own the hardware, the scaling, the patching, and the model quality ceiling.

The data-handling patterns that actually protect you

Whichever deployment you choose, the deployment model alone isn't a data protection strategy. It's the foundation. On top of it, a handful of concrete patterns do the real work:

  • Redaction before the model sees anything. Strip or mask names, account numbers, and other identifiers before they enter a prompt, and reattach them afterward. The model never needs to know who a record belongs to to help you classify, summarize, or draft from it.
  • Retrieval boundaries. If you're grounding the model in your own documents, enforce the same access controls on retrieval that you already enforce elsewhere. An LLM with retrieval should never be able to surface a document the requesting user couldn't otherwise open.
  • No-training guarantees, verified, not assumed. "We don't train on your data" is a claim, not a control. Get it in the contract, confirm the account-level setting that enforces it, and check that it's still true after any plan or vendor change.
  • Retention limits and audit logging. Set a defined window for how long requests and responses are stored, on both your side and the provider's, and log enough to reconstruct what was sent and returned if you're ever asked.

None of these are exotic. They're the same discipline you'd apply to any third-party integration that touches sensitive data — applied consistently, because an LLM makes it easy to forget you're still sending data somewhere.

How to choose without guessing

Start from the data, not the technology. Classify what the use case actually touches — public, internal, confidential, or regulated — and let that classification pick the deployment tier, rather than picking the most capable model and hoping the data will be fine.

Option Data control Setup effort Cost profile
Hosted API (with DPA) Contractual — data leaves, governed by agreement Low — days, not months Usage-based, predictable at small scale
Private / VPC-hosted Network-level — stays inside your cloud tenancy Moderate — cloud config, networking Usage plus infrastructure overhead
Self-hosted (open weights) Full — nothing leaves your infrastructure High — hosting, scaling, tuning, patching Fixed infrastructure cost, regardless of volume

For most business use cases — drafting, summarizing internal documents, classifying support tickets — a hosted API with a proper data processing agreement is genuinely sufficient, and it's usually the fastest path from idea to production. Reach for private or self-hosted deployment when the data classification, a specific regulation, or a client contract requires it, not by default. The expensive option isn't automatically the safer one; it's the appropriate one for a narrower set of cases.

The deployment model is the foundation. Redaction, retrieval boundaries, and verified no-training guarantees are what actually do the protecting on top of it.

What matters most is that the decision gets made on purpose. Write down the data classification, the deployment tier it maps to, and the handling patterns you're applying — then build. Teams that skip this step don't move faster; they just have the conversation later, usually after something has already shipped, which is a much harder time to have it.
