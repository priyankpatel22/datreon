LLMs Behind the Firewall: Integrating AI Without Leaking Your Data

Datreon · September 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Every security-conscious leader wants the same two things from AI: the productivity gains, and no new copy of sensitive data sitting on someone else's servers indefinitely. That trade-off isn't as forced as it looks. It just requires choosing your architecture on purpose instead of by default.

The question that stalls most AI initiatives isn't "does this model work." Language models are good enough, for most business tasks, to be useful today. The question that actually stalls things — and should — is narrower and harder: where does our data go once we send it, who can see it there, does it get used to train someone else's model, and what happens to it under a breach, a subpoena, or an audit.

Those are answerable questions. But they get answered by the deployment model you choose, not by which vendor has the best demo. Get the architecture right and the rest — redaction, retrieval boundaries, contract language — falls into place around it.

Two shapes your data flow can take

Strip away the marketing and every LLM integration reduces to one of two shapes: your data leaves your boundary to reach the model, or the model comes to your data instead. Everything else is a variation on which side of that line you're standing.

In practice, that spectrum breaks into three concrete options:

  • A hosted API, under a data processing agreement. You call a commercial model over the internet, but the contract specifies no training on your inputs, defined retention limits, and where the data is processed. Fast to adopt; your protection is contractual, not physical.
  • A private or VPC-hosted model. The same class of model runs inside your cloud tenant, or a dedicated instance a vendor operates on your behalf. Requests never touch shared, public infrastructure. More setup, meaningfully more control.
  • A self-hosted open model. You run the weights yourself, on your own infrastructure or air-gapped hardware. Nothing leaves you, ever, by construction rather than by promise. It also means you now own the operational burden a vendor used to carry.

The controls that matter more than the vendor's logo

Picking a deployment model gets you most of the way there, but three data-handling patterns matter regardless of which one you choose — and teams that skip them stay exposed even on the "safest" option.

  • Redact before it leaves the app. Strip or tokenize names, account numbers, and other identifiers in your own code before a prompt is assembled — not after. If a field never enters the request, it can't be logged, cached, or trained on downstream.
  • Scope retrieval to what the requester is allowed to see. A retrieval-augmented system is only as private as its permission model. If the search layer can pull any document into any answer, the model becomes a way to bypass access controls you already built elsewhere.
  • Get the guarantees in writing, not in a blog post. "We don't train on customer data" is a marketing sentence until it's a clause in a signed agreement with retention windows, deletion rights, and audit language attached.
The safest LLM deployment usually isn't the most expensive one. It's the one whose data boundary you could actually draw on a whiteboard.

How to choose without overthinking it

The three options aren't ranked best to worst — they trade control for convenience in a fairly predictable way. A hosted API asks the least of your team and the most of your trust. Self-hosting asks the opposite.

Three factors settle it in practice: how sensitive the data actually is (a public FAQ bot and a claims-processing system don't belong on the same deployment), how much your own team can realistically operate, and whether latency or volume push you toward infrastructure you control directly. Most organizations land on a private or VPC-hosted model for anything touching regulated or proprietary data, and reserve a hosted API for genuinely low-sensitivity work.

It's worth separating the question by workload rather than deciding once for the whole organization. A customer-facing chatbot answering questions from a public knowledge base carries very different risk than an internal tool summarizing contracts or medical records. Many teams end up running more than one deployment model at once — a hosted API for the low-stakes, high-volume work, and a private or self-hosted model reserved for anything that touches regulated or proprietary data. That split is normal, not a sign the strategy is unfinished.

It also pays to revisit the decision as the workload matures. A proof of concept built on a hosted API to move fast can graduate to a private deployment once it proves out and starts touching real customer data — the integration code barely changes, because the model's interface looks the same regardless of where it's running. What changes is everything around it: the network path, the contract, and who's accountable for what happens if something goes wrong.

Model Data control Cost profile Operational effort
Hosted API + DPA Contractual, not physical Usage-based, low upfront Minimal — mostly integration work
Private / VPC-hosted Isolated within your tenant Reserved capacity + integration Moderate — provisioning, access policy
Self-hosted open model Never leaves your infrastructure Hardware/compute, no per-call fee High — hosting, scaling, model upkeep

None of this requires slowing AI adoption to a crawl. It requires deciding, before the first prompt is ever sent, which of these three boxes your data is allowed to sit in — and then building redaction, retrieval boundaries, and contract terms around that answer instead of hoping the vendor handled it. Teams that skip that step don't usually get burned by the model being wrong. They get burned by data going somewhere nobody meant it to.
