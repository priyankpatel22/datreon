LLMs Behind the Firewall: Integrating AI Without Leaking Your Data

Datreon · August 31, 2026 · 7 min read

"We can't use AI on that data" is the fastest way a good idea dies in a security review. Most of the time, the objection is really about one unanswered question — where does the data actually go — and that question has real, well-understood answers.

Every AI conversation with a regulated or security-conscious buyer eventually reaches the same wall. Legal, procurement, or the security team asks a version of "does this send our data to a third party," nobody in the room can answer precisely, and the project stalls — not because the use case was bad, but because nobody had mapped where the data goes before someone asked.

That instinct isn't paranoia. Contracts, health records, personnel files, proprietary source code, and client communications are exactly the kind of data an organization is obligated to protect. The good news is that "use an LLM" and "send sensitive data to an unknown third party" are not the same decision. They just get treated that way when nobody has laid out the options.

Where your data actually goes

Underneath every AI integration is one architectural fork: does the model run outside your boundary, or inside it? Everything else — contracts, controls, cost — follows from that one choice.

Neither pattern is automatically wrong. A vendor API under a solid data-processing agreement is a perfectly reasonable choice for plenty of workloads. The mistake is not choosing deliberately — building on whichever option a developer happened to sign up for first, without anyone confirming it fits the data involved.

Three ways to deploy an LLM

In practice, almost every integration falls into one of three buckets. They trade off control, cost, and operational effort in predictable ways.

Option Data control Relative cost Operational effort
Hosted API, with a data-processing agreement Contractual — no training on your data, defined retention, vendor's infrastructure Low, usage-based Low — mostly reviewing the contract terms
Private / VPC-hosted model Strong — dedicated instance inside a private network, vendor manages the model Moderate to high, often fixed capacity Moderate — network and access configuration
Self-hosted open model Full — the model runs entirely inside infrastructure you control Highest upfront, GPU and ops cost High — you own hosting, scaling, and updates

Most organizations don't need to pick just one. A common, sensible pattern is a hosted API under a strong agreement for general work, reserved for a self-hosted or VPC-hosted model when the data is regulated, classified, or contractually restricted from leaving the building at all. It's also worth revisiting the choice as a project matures — a prototype built against a hosted API can graduate to a private deployment once the workload and the data it touches are better understood, instead of trying to guess the right answer on day one.

The data-handling patterns that actually matter

The deployment option sets the ceiling on how safe an integration can be. Three specific practices determine whether you actually get there.

No-training guarantees, in writing. Most enterprise-tier API agreements now state that your inputs and outputs are not used to train the underlying model and are retained only for a limited, defined window — sometimes zero. This is the single most important line in the contract, and it should be read, not assumed.

Redaction before the request leaves your system. For a lot of workloads, the model doesn't need the sensitive value itself — it needs the shape of the task. Names, account numbers, and identifiers can be tokenized or masked before the call and mapped back afterward, so the data that actually crosses the network edge is far less sensitive than what a person would see.

Retrieval boundaries. When a system retrieves internal documents to ground a model's answer (a common pattern for internal search and Q&A), the retrieval layer should enforce the same permissions the document already has. A model should never be able to surface a document to someone who couldn't already open it directly — the AI layer doesn't get its own access rules.

The question worth asking isn't "is this AI system secure?" It's "does this system enforce the same boundaries our data already had?" If the answer is yes, most of the fear disappears.

How to choose

Start from the data, not the technology. Classify what the workload actually touches — public, internal, confidential, or regulated — before picking an architecture. A marketing team drafting blog posts and a claims team processing medical records have no business making the same infrastructure decision, even if they're both "using AI."

From there, the choice mostly makes itself: public and internal data can usually move fast on a hosted API with a solid agreement. Confidential data deserves redaction and a closer look at the contract terms. Regulated or classified data belongs behind your own boundary — private, VPC-hosted, or fully self-hosted — where the question of where the data goes has a one-word answer: nowhere.

None of this requires slowing AI adoption to a crawl. It requires doing the classification once, up front, so the security review isn't a surprise at the end of the project but a checklist you already satisfied on the way in. Teams that treat data classification as the first step of the project, rather than a gate at the end of it, tend to move faster overall — not slower — because they stop re-litigating the same "can we even do this" conversation with every new stakeholder who walks in.

The organizations getting the most value from AI right now aren't the ones with access to a more powerful model. They're the ones who did the unglamorous work of matching the deployment pattern to the data, once, and then let their teams build on top of it with confidence instead of second-guessing every request.
